# Further reading: what should count as AI adoption success?

**Reviewed:** 29 October 2026. This is a reading note, not a new assessment method or an endorsement of another site's business-model taxonomy.

[AI Business Models](https://www.businessmodels.ai/) analyses eight ways AI products are sold and delivered. Its emphasis on **what to test first** and **what to watch first** is useful for workplace adoption, but the site primarily analyses **vendors' business economics**, not independent evidence of whether an employer should deploy AI.

The three most relevant articles are below. The links point to the original author's pages, rather than copied text or diagrams.

| Article | What the author focuses on | Useful adaptation for a workplace pilot | Important difference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [AI add-on to existing SaaS](https://www.businessmodels.ai/ai-add-on/) | Weekly AI usage, adoption of paid add-ons, and movement from pilot to wider rollout | Compare people **given access**, people **trying** the feature, and people **using it repeatedly for suitable tasks**. Investigate whether repeat use actually helps | Weekly usage is a vendor adoption measure, not proof of quality, value, consent, or readiness to expand |
| [Outcome-priced AI agents](https://www.businessmodels.ai/ai-outcome-pricing/) | Whether the customer and vendor agree what counts as a completed outcome, plus disputed results and recontact | Define a successful **end-to-end outcome before testing**. Have two people independently classify a small sample, including failures, handoffs, rework and complaints | A billable resolution can differ from a genuinely resolved user problem; a metric chosen for pricing may reward the wrong behaviour |
| [AI-native services](https://www.businessmodels.ai/ai-native-services/) | AI-assisted delivery where humans review work, and errors can affect trust and repeat use | Count human review, corrections, errors reaching others, support cost and time to finish the **whole job** | A service provider's margin or repeat-purchase rate is not the same as an internal team's productivity, safety or public benefit |

These are *adaptations proposed by this repository*, not tests run by the cited author or demonstrated improvements from our own guides. The articles include company examples and figures gathered from further sources. Verify an original source, its date, population and definitions before citing a numerical adoption or productivity claim. This note deliberately does not import such figures.

## A practical measurement example (fictional)

An internal team tries an AI assistant to summarise incoming requests.

| What is counted | Why it helps | What it cannot prove on its own |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Staff with access and weekly users | Whether the assistant is being tried and revisited | Whether it is worth using or appropriate for the work |
| Correct, accepted summaries out of a representative sample | Whether outputs meet an agreed standard | Whether the sample captures difficult and unusual cases |
| Minutes per completed request, including review and repair | Whether the **whole process** improved compared with a fair baseline | Whether the result would persist across a different period or workload |
| Errors that reached another person and time to recover | Whether failures are contained and the real cost of review | Whether rare high-severity events have been ruled out |
| Requests reopened or corrected after acceptance | Whether an apparently completed job stayed completed | Whether all downstream consequences are visible |

There is **no universal pass score** here. The relevant threshold depends on the workflow, people affected, acceptable errors, current permissions and who is responsible for decisions.

## Test first, then watch

A useful question to ask **before** a workplace pilot is: *What exactly would count as one successful completed job, including human checking and any later rework?* Ask a process owner and a reviewer to apply the proposed definition to the same few examples. Record disagreement rather than silently choosing the more flattering number.

A useful question to ask **during** a pilot is: *Are the quality, full-process time and exceptions improving together, or are we simply generating more AI activity?*

This extends the [existing guide to evaluating AI use](../guides/how-to-tell-whether-ai-actually-helped.md). It does not replace the [Pilot Readiness Assessment](https://shaunmarsden.github.io/practical-ai-adoption/pilot-readiness/), whose conditions include business results, acceptable quality, ownership, permission, oversight and recovery. Neither document grants operational approval.
