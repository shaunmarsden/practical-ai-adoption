# How to Tell Whether AI Actually Helped

**Start here:** Copy the brief below, describe how AI has been used on a real task, and paste it into the AI tool you already use.

```text
I want an honest assessment of whether this AI trial or ongoing use actually worked, not just whether it felt like it did.

What we tried:
[Describe the task and how AI has been used on it.]

What we have so far:
[Paste any time estimates, logs, feedback, or examples of output, including anything that went wrong.]

Assess this based on:
- what was actually measured versus what is being assumed or inferred;
- whether any "before" comparison is a fair one (same period, same workload, same conditions);
- whether the full task time was captured, not only the AI's part of it;
- how representative any logged sample is of the full volume;
- what any known quality misses imply about risk at full scale;
- whether anything about how the trial was run could have inflated the result temporarily, for example people knowing they were being watched;
- what would need to be true, or measured, before this becomes a confident decision.

Give me a plain recommendation, but be explicit about what the current evidence does and does not support.

Do not assume a percentage improvement that isn't actually in the data. Do not treat enthusiasm or a good feeling as proof of an outcome.
```

A feeling that AI helped is not the same as evidence that it helped. Both can be true at once, and telling them apart is the point of this guide.

## Why this needs its own check

- **Nine in ten executives saw no measurable impact.** A survey of nearly 6,000 senior executives across the US, UK, Germany and Australia found this over the previous three years, despite most of their firms actively using AI.
- **Believed gains are bigger than measured ones.** Separate research on CFO-reported outcomes documents a "productivity paradox": the productivity gains people believe AI delivered are consistently larger than the gains actually implied by revenue and employment figures.

Neither finding says AI cannot help. They say that a positive impression, on its own, is weak evidence of a real outcome. That gap is exactly where this guide is aimed.

Sources are listed at the bottom of this guide.

## What a fair check usually needs

- **A real baseline.** Something measured before AI was used, from a comparable period, workload and set of conditions. A baseline from a much quieter or busier time is not comparable, even if it is the only number available.
- **The full task time, not just the AI's part.** Generating a draft is often the fastest step. Reviewing, correcting and sending it is usually where the real time goes, and it is the step most likely to be skipped when people report how "fast" something felt.
- **A sample that represents the whole job, not just the easy parts.** If only some cases got logged in detail, ask which ones were skipped and why. People tend to have time to log the simple cases and no time to log the hard ones, which quietly tilts the evidence toward a win.
- **An honest look at what went wrong, not only what went well.** One clear mistake, especially in a sensitive or regulated area, can matter more than a dozen smooth cases. Ask how the mistake was found, and how long it took.
- **Awareness that being watched changes behaviour.** People tend to work more carefully, and report more positively, while they know a trial is being closely monitored. That effect fades once something becomes routine.
- **Comfort with a null result.** If a careful check finds no material difference, that is a legitimate outcome, not a failed test. Treating "no difference found" as disappointing, rather than as useful information, is exactly what pushes people toward inflating a weak signal into a confident claim.

## What to watch for

- **A leading question dressed up as a request for evidence.** "Can you write up a recommendation confirming we should roll this out" is a different question from "did this actually work," even when the same data sits underneath it. Answer the second question first.
- **Social proof standing in for your own evidence.** Another team's success, or a manager's enthusiasm, tells you very little about your own error rate, your own workload, or your own review process.
- **A single time figure doing too much work.** "It only took a minute" is often true and still misleading, if that minute was one step in a longer process.
- **A mistake being treated as a footnote.** If a real error reached a real person before anyone caught it, that is information about what the current safety net actually catches, not an unlucky one-off to set aside.
