# How to Tell Whether AI Actually Helped

**Start here:** Copy the brief below, describe how you've used AI on a real task, and paste it into the AI tool you already use.

```text
I want an honest assessment of whether this AI trial or ongoing use actually worked, not just whether it felt like it did.

What we tried:
[Describe the task and how AI has been used on it.]

What we have so far:
[Paste any time estimates, logs, feedback, or examples of output, including anything that went wrong.]

Assess this based on:
- what was actually measured versus what is being assumed or inferred;
- whether any "before" comparison is a fair one (same period, same workload, same conditions);
- whether the full task time was captured, not only the AI's part of it;
- how representative any logged sample is of the full volume;
- what any known quality misses imply about risk at full scale;
- whether anything about how the trial was run could have inflated the result temporarily, for example people knowing they were being watched;
- what would need to be true, or measured, before this becomes a confident decision.

Give me a plain recommendation, but be explicit about what the current evidence does and does not support.

Do not assume a percentage improvement that isn't actually in the data. Do not treat enthusiasm or a good feeling as proof of an outcome.
```

Feeling that AI helped isn't the same as evidence that it did. Both can be true at once. This guide is about telling them apart.

## Why this needs its own check

Nine in ten executives saw no measurable impact. A survey of nearly 6,000 senior executives in the US, UK, Germany and Australia found this over the previous three years, even though most of their firms were using AI.

People believe the gains are bigger than they are. Separate research on what CFOs report describes a "productivity paradox": the gains people believe AI brought are consistently larger than the ones revenue and employment figures suggest.

Neither finding says AI can't help. They say a good impression, on its own, is weak evidence of a real result. This guide is aimed at that gap.

The sources are at the bottom of this guide.

## What a fair check usually needs

A real starting point. You need something measured before AI was used, from a similar period, workload and set of conditions. A figure from a much quieter or busier time isn't comparable, even if it's the only one you have.

The whole task time, not just the AI's part. Writing the draft is often the quickest step. Reviewing, correcting and sending it is usually where the time goes, and it's the step people leave out when they say how "fast" something felt.

A sample that covers the whole job, not just the easy parts. If only some cases were logged in detail, ask which were skipped and why. People have time to log the simple cases and no time to log the hard ones, and that tilts the evidence towards a win.

A look at what went wrong, not only what went well. One clear mistake, especially in a sensitive or regulated area, can matter more than a dozen smooth cases. Ask how the mistake was found and how long that took.

Knowing that being watched changes how people work. People work more carefully, and report more positively, while they know a trial is being watched closely. That wears off once the work becomes routine.

Being ready for no difference. If a careful check finds no real difference, that's a fair result, not a failed test. Treating "no difference found" as a let-down, rather than as useful information, is what pushes people to turn a weak signal into a confident claim.

## What to watch for

A leading question dressed up as a request for evidence. "Can you write up a recommendation confirming we should roll this out" is a different question from "did this actually work," even when the same data sits underneath. Answer the second question first.

Other people's success standing in for your own evidence. Another team's results, or a manager's enthusiasm, tell you very little about your own error rate, workload or review process.

One time figure carrying too much weight. "It only took a minute" is often true and still misleading, if that minute was one step in a longer process.

A mistake treated as a footnote. If a real error reached a real person before anyone caught it, that tells you what your checks catch now. It isn't an unlucky one-off to set aside.
