# Why AI Projects Stall After the Demo Works

**Start here:** Copy the brief below, describe a rollout that has quietly stalled, and paste it into the AI tool you already use.

```text
I want to understand why adoption of this AI tool or workflow stalled after a promising demo or early trial, not just accept the first explanation offered.

What happened:
[Describe the demo or trial, what it showed, and what has happened since.]

What we know now:
[Paste any usage figures, feedback, complaints, or observations, including anything that sounds like a simple explanation.]

Assess this based on:
- what the evidence actually shows about output quality, versus what a few people assumed or reported;
- whether the people who kept using it report the same problem as the people who stopped;
- what happened, or did not happen, to support adoption after the demo, such as training, a named owner, or updates to the normal process;
- whether people have a clear, consistent understanding of how using it affects how their work or performance is judged;
- whether anyone has actually been tracking adoption or outcomes, or whether this is the first time anyone has looked;
- what a demo can and cannot tell you about whether something will actually get used afterwards.

Give me your best explanation for why adoption stalled, and be explicit about which parts of that explanation the evidence actually supports, and which parts would need more checking.

Do not assume the tool's output quality is the problem just because a few people said so. Do not recommend a replacement without first ruling out the alternative explanations.
```

A demo shows that AI can do something. It does not show that people will keep doing it, or that the organisation has made it easy to keep doing it.

## Why this needs its own check

- **Gartner predicts abandonment on two separate fronts.** Through 2026, organisations will abandon 60% of AI projects that are not supported by AI-ready data; separately, 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, driven by poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs or unclear business value.
- **BCG found most companies are not getting value at all.** Its 2025 research across more than 1,250 companies found 60% are not achieving material value from AI at all, and only 5% are achieving value at scale, despite most leaders ranking AI a top priority.

None of these findings say the underlying models stopped working. They point at the same gap: what happens, or fails to happen, after a promising start.

Sources are listed at the bottom of this guide.

## What usually explains a stall

- **No named owner.** Someone ran the demo and got people interested. If nobody is responsible for what happens next, interest fades and nobody notices until someone happens to check.
- **No process integration.** If using the tool is optional and left to individual habit, it competes with the familiar way of doing things every single time, and usually loses under time pressure.
- **No training beyond the demo.** A demo shows what is possible in ideal conditions. It does not teach anyone what to do when the output is wrong, unclear or needs correcting, which is the moment that actually decides whether someone keeps using it.
- **Unclear effect on how work is judged.** If people are not told whether using the tool helps or hurts how their output or performance is measured, the safe, familiar option usually wins by default.
- **No one was watching.** If nobody tracked adoption after the demo, a stall can go unnoticed for months, and the first number anyone sees is a one-off snapshot, not a trend.

## What to watch for

- **Blaming the tool first.** A handful of early complaints about quality are easy to hear and easy to act on. Check whether the people who kept using it report the same problem before treating a complaint as the cause.
- **Small numbers doing a lot of work.** A few negative reports and a couple of positive ones is not enough evidence either way. Look at what is actually being compared before drawing a conclusion.
- **Treating a demo as proof of adoption.** A good demo proves capability, once, under attention and with an expert in the room. It says very little about ordinary use, months later, without support.
- **Reaching for a replacement too early.** Switching tools before ruling out ownership, process and incentive gaps usually reproduces the same stall with a different product.
