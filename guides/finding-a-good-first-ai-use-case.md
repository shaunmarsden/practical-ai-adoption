# Finding a Good First AI Use Case

**Start here:** Copy the checklist below, fill in the tasks you're thinking of trying, and paste it into the AI tool you already use.

```text
I want to choose one sensible first AI experiment from a list of tasks I could try.

Here are the tasks:
[For each task, say: what it involves, how often it happens, how long it takes, what could go wrong if the output is wrong, and whether the information is sensitive.]

Assess them based on:
- whether the task happens often enough and takes enough effort for improvement to matter;
- whether AI is actually suited to the kind of work, or whether a simpler rules-based automation would do it better;
- whether the information is appropriate to use;
- how easy the output is for me to check;
- what could happen if the output is wrong;
- whether the first experiment can stay small, reversible and under my control;
- what a fair baseline and success measure would look like.

Rank the tasks.

Then give me:
1. the one you would try first;
2. why it is a good first experiment;
3. why the others would not be your first choice;
4. the smallest useful trial;
5. what I should measure;
6. what I still need to check or decide.

Do not assume permissions, policies or facts I have not given you.
Do not assume AI is the right tool just because a task is repetitive.
```

Most people trying AI at work don't fail because the tool is weak. They fail because they chose the wrong first task. Either it was too risky to learn from safely, or it mattered so little that it proved nothing.

## Start with a job, not a feature

A good first use starts with work you already do, not with something you've read AI can do. If you can't name the task before you open the tool, notice that.

Two UK studies show this is a common problem, not a personal failing. They aren't the final word. The government's 2026 AI Adoption Research found that not having found a use for AI is one of the barriers UK businesses report most often. The Office for National Statistics found the same in its 2023 survey of UK firms: difficulty identifying activities or business use cases was the barrier to AI adoption they reported most. The sources are at the end of this guide.

## What a strong first experiment usually looks like

A good first task usually has most of these:

- It happens often enough to matter. A one-off task gives you one data point. A recurring one lets you judge whether AI helps.
- It suits what AI does well: making sense of messy language, drafting, summarising or spotting patterns in text. Not fixed, rule-based data entry.
- You already know what a good result looks like. If you can't judge the output, you can't learn from the trial.
- The information is suitable to use. Ordinary work content is an easier start than anything confidential or personal.
- It's small enough to try without new tools or connections. You should be able to run it alongside how you work now, not replace that on day one.
- A person still makes the call that matters. The trial shouldn't hand over a decision about a customer, a payment or someone's job.
- You can measure it against something. A rough idea of the time or quality now is enough. Without it, you can't tell whether the trial helped.

## What to watch for

A repetitive task isn't always an AI task. If the rules are fixed and there's no real judgement, a simple script, a macro or a feature in a system you already have will usually fit better, and be more reliable, than a language model.

A task can be too sensitive or too important for a first try, even if AI could help with part of it. Confidential personal data, financial approval and anything customers see are usually better as a second or third experiment, once you have a feel for how far to trust the output.

A task can be too easy to prove anything. If it takes minutes and nothing much hangs on it, a good result won't tell you much.

Watch for a vague idea of success. If you can't say in advance what "it worked" would look like, the trial won't settle anything.

## Try it on your own list

Write down two or three real tasks you do, with enough detail to judge them against the checklist: how often, how long, what a wrong answer would cost, and whether the information is sensitive. Then run the starter prompt at the top of this guide.

The [Marlowe & Birch example](../examples/marlowe-birch-first-use-case-example.md) tests this on six mixed tasks. One of them looks like a good AI task but isn't. [Read the review](../evaluations/marlowe-birch-first-use-case-review.md) for what the comparison did and didn't show.
